## 4-year outline (DreamCo-derived guidance, not O*NET data)

1. Year 1, foundations. General education, including English Language (technical writing: design documents, code-review comments, and README files for your own projects), plus deliberate practice in the top-rated skills: Critical Thinking (debugging by forming and testing hypotheses, and comparing algorithm trade-offs in problem sets); Reading Comprehension (reading API documentation, language references, and one research paper per term); Active Listening (pair programming and restating requirements back to project partners before coding).
2. Year 2, core knowledge. Build the highest-rated knowledge areas for this major: Computers and Electronics (programming fundamentals, data structures and algorithms, computer organization, and operating systems); Engineering and Technology (software engineering: requirements, testing, version control, and a team project with a real client); Mathematics (discrete mathematics, linear algebra, probability, and the theory of computation).
3. Year 3, depth toward the entry targets (Software Developers; Software Quality Assurance Analysts and Testers; Information Security Analysts): Design (software design: object-oriented and system design, design patterns, and user-interface prototyping); Telecommunications (computer networking: protocols, sockets programming, and how distributed systems communicate). Keep building Writing (writing clear commit messages, bug reports, and design proposals with peer review); Speaking (demoing projects at hackathons or class showcases and explaining code in technical interviews); Active Learning (picking up a new language or framework for each project and writing up what you learned) through project work.
4. Year 4, launch. Internship or capstone aimed at Software Developers; Software Quality Assurance Analysts and Testers; Information Security Analysts; build a portfolio from the practice tasks below that shows Computers and Electronics, Engineering and Technology.

Knowledge areas from the top list that the outline deliberately does not cover:

- Customer and Personal Service (3.02): service and client-relations knowledge; the outline leaves it to internships, part-time work and the practice tasks rather than a course slot.
- Administration and Management (2.89): management and planning knowledge matters most after several years of experience, so the outline gives it no course slot (a management elective is optional).
- Education and Training (2.76): teaching and training others is built through presenting, tutoring and peer teaching in projects rather than a dedicated course.

### 11.0701-P1. `15-1253.00` Software Quality Assurance Analysts and Testers, O*NET task 14638 (test plan)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Plan how a piece of software will be checked: which scenarios to exercise, which scripts or manual steps to run, and in what order.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. A student-run campus food-ordering app is about to ship a 'split the bill' feature that lets up to six friends share one order, each paying by card or meal-plan credit. Write the test plan for this feature: scope and out-of-scope items, at least eight named test scenarios (include rounding of odd cents, one payer's card being declined, and a friend leaving the group mid-checkout), which scenarios you would automate versus run by hand, test data you need, and the exit criteria for release.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Scenario coverage: Scenarios cover the normal path, boundary values (1 and 6 payers, odd-cent totals) and failure paths (declined card, timeout, member leaving), not only happy paths.
- Automation judgment: The split between automated and manual tests is justified by repeatability, risk and cost, and the test data needed is stated concretely.
- Release criteria: Exit criteria are measurable (e.g. zero open severity-1 defects, all payment-path tests passing) and say who signs off.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Scope statement naming the split-bill flow, payment providers and meal-plan ledger; excludes unrelated menu features.
- Scenario table with ID, preconditions, steps and expected result; includes the cent-rounding rule (who absorbs the leftover cent).
- Automates calculation and API-level payment tests; keeps exploratory and multi-device UX checks manual.
- Lists test accounts, sandbox cards that decline, and a meal-plan balance near zero.
- Exit criteria with severity thresholds and a named approver.

### 11.0701-P2. `15-1212.00` Information Security Analysts, O*NET task 5313 (training session plan)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Teach staff safe computing habits and build a culture of security awareness so the organization's systems stay protected and run smoothly.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. A 40-person food-bank nonprofit just had a volunteer coordinator's email account taken over through a fake 'shared document' link, and the attacker emailed donors asking for gift cards. Design a 30-minute security-awareness session for staff and regular volunteers: three learning objectives, the agenda minute by minute, one realistic hands-on exercise built from this incident (no real victim names), and how you will measure whether behavior changed 60 days later.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Audience fit: Content suits non-technical staff and volunteers, uses the real incident pattern, and avoids jargon or blame.
- Actionable habits: Objectives translate into specific behaviors (checking sender domains, reporting suspicious mail, using MFA) that the exercise actually practices.
- Measured follow-up: The 60-day measure is concrete, such as a simulated phishing click rate or report rate compared with a baseline.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Objectives: recognize credential-phishing cues, report quickly through one named channel, turn on MFA.
- Agenda: 5 min story of the incident, 10 min cue spotting, 10 min hands-on inbox triage exercise, 5 min MFA setup check.
- Exercise uses three sanitized sample emails, one legitimate and two malicious.
- Follow-up: simulated phish at day 45 to 60, compare click and report rates with a pre-session baseline.
- Notes privacy: individual results are not used for discipline.

### 11.0701-P3. `15-1252.00` Software Developers, O*NET task 21670 (change proposal)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Change existing software to fix defects, keep it working on new platforms or interfaces, and make it faster.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. Your registrar's office runs a five-year-old Python script that exports course enrollments to a CSV for the state reporting portal. Since enrollment doubled it takes 40 minutes and times out, it crashes on student names with accented characters, and the portal is moving from CSV upload to a JSON API next term. Write a change proposal: diagnose the likely causes of each problem, the code changes you would make, how you will prove the new version produces the same records as the old one, and a rollback plan.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Diagnosis: Each symptom gets a plausible technical cause (e.g. row-by-row queries, wrong text encoding) and a way to confirm it, such as profiling or a failing test case.
- Sound modification: Changes are scoped and safe: performance fix, UTF-8 handling, and an output layer that supports both CSV and the new API without rewriting everything.
- Verification and rollback: Equivalence is proven with a record-level comparison on real-sized data, and rollback is concrete.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Profile first; likely N+1 database queries or loading everything into memory; fix with batched queries or a single join.
- Encoding crash: read and write explicitly as UTF-8; add test names with accents and non-Latin scripts.
- Separate data extraction from output formatting so a JSON API client can be added beside the CSV writer.
- Verification: run old and new on the same snapshot and diff record counts and field values.
- Rollback: keep the old script deployable and switch back with one configuration flag.

### 11.0701-P4. `15-1253.00` Software Quality Assurance Analysts and Testers, O*NET task 14639 (regression test checklist)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Check that changes to a system work correctly and do not break anything before they are released.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. A web app used by 3,000 club members is replacing its password-hashing library (from an old salted SHA-1 scheme to bcrypt) and will rehash each password when that user next logs in. Before deployment, write the regression checklist you would run: what must still work, the specific edge cases for users who have not logged in since the change, the security checks, the performance check on the login endpoint, and the go/no-go rule.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Change-specific risks: Covers migration edge cases: old-hash users, users mid-reset, accounts with very long or Unicode passwords, and the first login after deploy.
- Measurable checks: Each item has a pass condition, such as login latency under a stated threshold or no plaintext passwords in logs.
- Decision rule: A clear go/no-go rule with what happens if a check fails, including a rollback path that does not lock users out.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Core regressions: login, logout, password reset, account creation, session expiry.
- Migration cases: legacy-hash user logs in and is rehashed; reset link issued before deploy still works or fails safely.
- Security: confirm the bcrypt cost factor, no hashes or passwords in logs, rate limiting still on.
- Performance: measure p95 login latency before and after; bcrypt cost tuned to stay within target.
- Go/no-go: all severity-1 checks pass; rollback keeps both hash verifiers so no user is locked out.

### 11.0701-P5. `15-1212.00` Information Security Analysts, O*NET task 5318 (implementation coordination plan)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Coordinate how a computer-security plan is put into place, working with the organization's own staff and with outside vendors.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. A three-office dental practice has bought two things: multi-factor authentication for its practice-management software, and a managed endpoint-protection service from an outside vendor. You are the security analyst coordinating the rollout over four weeks without disrupting patient scheduling. Write the coordination plan: who does what (practice staff, office managers, the MFA vendor, the endpoint vendor), the week-by-week schedule, how you will communicate with front-desk staff, and the three biggest rollout risks with mitigations.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Roles and handoffs: Responsibilities of internal staff and each vendor are explicit, including who approves changes and who provides support at each office.
- Low-disruption schedule: Sequencing protects clinic operations: pilot first, after-hours installs, and no MFA enforcement on a full patient day without fallback.
- Risk handling: Risks are specific (lost phones, legacy imaging PCs, vendor delays) and each has a workable mitigation.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- RACI-style table: analyst coordinates, office managers schedule staff, vendors install and support.
- Week 1 pilot at one office; weeks 2 and 3 roll out to the others; week 4 enforce MFA and review.
- Front desk gets a one-page guide and a named on-call contact during the first enforced day.
- Risks: shared front-desk logins, old imaging workstations unsupported by the agent, phones unavailable; mitigations include hardware tokens and exceptions logged with an expiry date.
- Success measures: percentage of accounts enrolled and endpoint agents reporting.

### 11.0701-P6. `15-1252.00` Software Developers, O*NET task 21662 (feasibility memo)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Study what users need and what the software must do, then judge whether a design can be delivered within the available time and budget.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. The university library asks for a study-room booking app: students reserve rooms from their phones, staff can block rooms for events, no-shows are released after 15 minutes, and it must work with campus single sign-on. You have two part-time student developers for eight weeks and no budget for paid services beyond existing campus hosting. Write a one-page feasibility memo: the requirements you would confirm with users, a rough effort estimate per feature, what fits in eight weeks, what you would cut or defer, and your recommendation.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Requirements analysis: Distinguishes must-have from nice-to-have requirements and names open questions to confirm with students and staff.
- Credible estimate: Effort estimates are broken down by feature, include testing and SSO integration risk, and add up within the stated capacity.
- Clear recommendation: Recommends a specific scope (build, reduce, or buy/adapt existing tool) with the trade-offs stated.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Must-haves: SSO login, view availability, book and cancel, staff block-out; nice-to-have: notifications, analytics.
- Open questions: booking limits per student, accessibility needs, room check-in method for no-show release.
- Estimate in developer-weeks per feature, with SSO and testing called out; total compared with about 16 part-time developer-weeks.
- Defer push notifications and analytics; consider an existing open-source booking system if SSO support exists.
- Recommendation with a scope, a timeline and the main risk.
