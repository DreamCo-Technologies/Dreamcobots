## 4-year outline (DreamCo-derived guidance, not O*NET data)

1. Year 1, foundations. General education, including English Language (writing documentation, metadata standards, and user-facing help content), plus deliberate practice in the top-rated skills: Reading Comprehension (reading standards documents, such as metadata and accessibility guidelines, and summarizing them); Critical Thinking (evaluating information sources and comparing system options against requirements); Active Listening (conducting user interviews and confirming needs before proposing systems).
2. Year 2, core knowledge. Build the highest-rated knowledge areas for this major: Computers and Electronics (programming, databases, networking, and information systems); Engineering and Technology (systems analysis and design, and information architecture); Mathematics (statistics for data analysis and discrete mathematics).
3. Year 3, depth toward the entry targets (Software Developers; Web and Digital Interface Designers; Document Management Specialists): Design (information architecture and user-experience design: site maps, wireframes, and usability testing); Sales and Marketing (digital analytics and information products: audience research, web metrics, and content strategy). Keep building Writing (producing requirement documents, user guides, and usability reports); Active Learning (learning a new data or web tool each term through self-directed tutorials); Speaking (presenting system recommendations to non-technical stakeholders) through project work.
4. Year 4, launch. Internship or capstone aimed at Software Developers; Web and Digital Interface Designers; Document Management Specialists; build a portfolio from the practice tasks below that shows Computers and Electronics, Engineering and Technology.

Knowledge areas from the top list that the outline deliberately does not cover:

- Customer and Personal Service (3.11): service and client-relations knowledge; the outline leaves it to internships, part-time work and the practice tasks rather than a course slot.
- Administration and Management (2.92): management and planning knowledge matters most after several years of experience, so the outline gives it no course slot (a management elective is optional).
- Education and Training (2.71): teaching and training others is built through presenting, tutoring and peer teaching in projects rather than a dedicated course.

### 11.0401-P1. `15-1299.03` Document Management Specialists, O*NET task 16232 (classification taxonomy draft)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Help build a scheme for classifying documents and content so information is easy to capture, search and retrieve.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. A mid-sized city's planning department has 120,000 scanned and born-digital files (permits, site plans, council memos, public comments, inspection photos) sitting in shared drives with inconsistent folder names. Draft a classification taxonomy and metadata scheme for a new content-management system: top-level categories and two levels below, five to eight required metadata fields with controlled values, how retention and public-records status are captured, and how you would test the scheme with real staff searches.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Usable structure: Categories are mutually exclusive enough to file consistently and reflect how planning staff actually look things up (by parcel, project, document type).
- Metadata quality: Required fields use controlled vocabularies or formats (parcel ID pattern, dates), and public-records and retention status are captured.
- User testing: Proposes testing with real tasks, such as staff filing samples or finding items, and measuring agreement or time to find.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Top level by function (permits, plans, meetings, enforcement, public input), then project or parcel, then document type.
- Required metadata: parcel ID, project number, document type, date, author unit, public-records flag, retention category.
- Controlled lists for document type; validation pattern for parcel IDs.
- Card-sort or tree-test with about ten staff; measure filing agreement and search success.
- Plan for migrating legacy folders with automated suggestions plus manual review.

### 11.0401-P2. `15-1252.00` Software Developers, O*NET task 21661 (system recommendation memo)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Study the available information to decide whether to install a new system or change an existing one, then recommend and plan that work.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. A regional food co-op tracks member volunteer hours in a spreadsheet shared by five coordinators; hours are lost, duplicate member records exist, and the board wants monthly reports. Gather what you would need to know and write a recommendation memo: current-state problems, three options (fix the spreadsheet with structure and validation, adopt an off-the-shelf volunteer tool, or build a small web app), a comparison on cost, effort, data quality and maintenance, your recommendation, and a rollout plan for the chosen option.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Current-state analysis: Problems are traced to causes (no unique member ID, free-text entry, concurrent editing) using evidence the student says they would collect.
- Fair option comparison: Options are compared on consistent criteria including who maintains the solution after the student leaves.
- Actionable plan: The recommendation includes data cleanup, migration, training and a check that reports match after cutover.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Interview coordinators and sample the sheet to count duplicates and gaps.
- Option table: cost, setup time, data validation, reporting, maintenance burden, privacy.
- Likely recommendation: an off-the-shelf tool or structured form plus database, since a custom build has no long-term maintainer.
- Migration: deduplicate members on name plus email, import historical hours, reconcile totals.
- Train coordinators; run the old and new systems in parallel for one month.

### 11.0401-P3. `15-1255.00` Web and Digital Interface Designers, O*NET task 21686 (handoff and collaboration plan)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Work with front-end and back-end developers so a website project is delivered completely.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. You designed the new course-catalog website for a community college: search with filters, course detail pages, and a 'plan my semester' view. Two front-end developers and one back-end developer start building next week. Write the collaboration plan: what you hand off (components, states, responsive breakpoints, accessibility notes), which decisions you need from the back-end developer (search API fields, response times), how design questions get answered during the build, and how you review the built pages against the design.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Complete handoff: Handoff covers interaction states (empty, loading, error), responsive behavior and accessibility, not just static mockups.
- Cross-role dependencies: Identifies what design needs from the back end (data fields, filter values, pagination) and flags design choices that affect performance.
- Review loop: Defines a regular review routine with acceptance criteria for design fidelity and accessibility.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Component inventory with states and tokens (colors, spacing, type) in a shared design file.
- Breakpoints for phone, tablet and desktop; keyboard and screen-reader notes for filters.
- Questions for back end: which fields are searchable, filter counts, latency target, handling of sections with no seats.
- Twice-weekly design check-ins and a shared question log with owners.
- Review each page against acceptance criteria, including WCAG contrast and focus order.

### 11.0401-P4. `15-1299.03` Document Management Specialists, O*NET task 16231 (needs assessment plan)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Find out what document-management capabilities a department or its users actually need.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. A hospital's research office wants a document-management system for study protocols, consent-form versions, and regulatory correspondence; three research teams currently use email attachments and personal drives. Write a needs-assessment plan and a sample findings summary: who you will talk to, the questions you will ask, which existing documents you will inventory, how you will capture compliance needs (version control, audit trail, retention), and a prioritized list of requirements written as clearly invented example findings.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Right stakeholders: Includes coordinators, investigators, regulatory staff, IT and records management, not only managers.
- Method: Combines interviews, a document inventory, and observation of a real workflow such as a consent-form amendment.
- Prioritized requirements: Findings are written as testable requirements and ranked by risk and frequency; example findings are labeled as invented.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Stakeholder list and a 30-minute interview guide on current workflow, pain points and must-not-lose items.
- Inventory of document types, volume, and where the authoritative version lives.
- Compliance needs: version history, approval trail, controlled access, retention schedule.
- Requirements such as: only the current approved consent version can be downloaded for enrollment.
- Ranked by risk (regulatory findings) and frequency of use.

### 11.0401-P5. `15-1252.00` Software Developers, O*NET task 21667 (design document with model)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Design, build and change software systems, relying on quantitative models and analysis to forecast how a design will behave.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. A university wants a notification service that texts students when a seat opens in a full course section. Write a short design document: the components (enrollment feed, waitlist store, notifier, rate limiter), a simple queueing or load model that estimates peak messages per minute during registration week from stated assumptions, how that estimate drives design choices, and how you would measure in production whether the model was right.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Coherent design: Components and data flow are clear, including how duplicate or stale notifications are prevented.
- Quantitative model: The load estimate uses stated assumptions (students, sections, open-seat events per hour) and the arithmetic is shown and plausible.
- Model validation: Plans to compare predicted and observed load and adjust capacity or design if they diverge.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Event-driven design: enrollment change triggers a check of the waitlist and queues notifications.
- Assumptions such as 20,000 students and 3,000 full sections, with a peak of seat changes in the first hour; computes messages per minute.
- Result drives queue size, SMS provider rate limit and batching.
- Idempotency key per student and section to avoid duplicate texts; expiry if the seat is taken.
- Dashboards on queue depth and send rate; compare with the model after the first registration day.

### 11.0401-P6. `15-1255.00` Web and Digital Interface Designers, O*NET task 21713 (user guide and test protocol)

Work activity (DreamCo paraphrase): Write and edit technical documents for digital interface designs, such as user guides, test protocols and reports.

DreamCo-original practice prompt. Your team redesigned the online form residents use to request a bulky-trash pickup from the city. Write two short documents: (1) a one-page help guide for residents, written at about an eighth-grade reading level and available in plain language for translation, and (2) a usability-test protocol for five participants that names the tasks, what you will observe, success criteria and how findings will be reported to the development team.

Rubric (0 to 2 points each):

- Plain, accurate guide: The help guide matches the form's steps, uses plain language and short sentences, and covers common errors.
- Usable test protocol: Tasks are realistic, success criteria are observable, and the protocol includes consent and a think-aloud script.
- Reporting: Explains how findings are ranked by severity and turned into specific fixes for developers.

Reference answer outline (what a strong answer includes):

- Guide: what items qualify, step-by-step with screenshots placeholders, what happens after submitting, how to change a request.
- Readability check with a standard formula; avoid idioms to ease translation.
- Test tasks: request pickup for a couch, change the date, find out why a mattress is rejected.
- Measures: task completion, time, errors, and post-task ease rating.
- Report: severity-ranked issues, each with evidence and a recommended change.
